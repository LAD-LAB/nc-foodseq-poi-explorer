Durham, North Carolina

What food species are in your neighborhood's water?

NC FoodSeq Explorer uses DNA sequencing to identify food species detected in Durham's wastewater, offering a window into neighborhood-level food patterns.

How it works · in four steps

Collect

Wastewater samples are gathered from sites across Durham's sewer network.

Sequence

DNA in the samples is extracted and sequenced to identify genetic material.

Identify

Sequences are matched to known food species, both plants and animals.

Map

Results are mapped to neighborhoods so you can explore local food DNA signals.

The research behind it

From the Edible Atlas Lab at Duke

The Edible Atlas Lab, led by Lawrence David in the Department of Medicine at Duke University, studies food consumption patterns through wastewater-based epidemiology. FoodSeq uses environmental DNA sequencing to detect food species in municipal sewage. This explorer is a research prototype that maps those signals to Durham neighborhoods so communities can see what's flowing through their local sewer system.

The technology

What is FoodSeq?

Traditional methods for tracking what people eat rely on self-reporting: food diaries, recall surveys, questionnaires. These approaches miss roughly 20% of foods consumed due to memory gaps, and they tend to underrepresent low-income and minority populations.

FoodSeq takes a different approach. It uses DNA metabarcoding, a technique borrowed from ecology, to identify plant and animal species directly from biological samples. Two genetic markers do the work: trnL for plants and 12S for animals. Each sample is sequenced and matched against an open-source reference database of nearly 900 species.

The method has been validated in multiple studies. A 2025 review in mSystems ("From stool to sequence") describes the full methodology. Published applications include pediatric nutrition studies and dietary intervention trials.

For this explorer, FoodSeq is applied to wastewater. Samples collected from Durham's sewer network carry traces of food DNA from entire neighborhoods. This makes it possible to study community-level food signals without requiring anyone to participate individually.

In this city · how it works

From wastewater to a neighborhood map

STEP 01

Collect

Wastewater sampled from manholes and lift stations, each serving a known neighborhood.

STEP 02

Sequence

trnL (plants) & 12S (animals) markers extract and read the food DNA present.

STEP 03

Identify

Sequences matched to the reference database: rice, chicken, salmon, blueberries, and hundreds more.

STEP 04

Map

Joined to census demographics and USDA food-access data by service area.

Glossary

The terms, plainly

Metabarcoding
Sequencing a short, shared stretch of DNA from a mixed sample to identify every species present at once.
trnL plants
A chloroplast marker gene used to detect and tell apart plant foods in a sample.
12S animals
A mitochondrial marker gene used to detect and tell apart animal foods.
Amplicon sequencing
Copying (amplifying) one target DNA region many times, then reading it. This is the basis of FoodSeq.
Wastewater-based epidemiology
Reading community-level signals from what a population collectively flushes. Anonymous by design.
Service area
The neighborhood a given sampling point drains, used as the map's unit of analysis.
Reference database
The lab's open-source library of ~900 plant & vertebrate genes that names each sequence.
Food desert USDA
An area with limited access to affordable, nutritious food, per USDA's Food Access Research Atlas.
Ethics & privacy

Built to protect the people it studies

No individuals — ever

Wastewater is pooled across hundreds of households before it reaches a sampling point. Results describe neighborhoods, never a person or address.

No participation required

Unlike surveys, no one opts in, fills anything out, or is identifiable. That's what lets FoodSeq reach groups traditional methods miss.

Correlation, not proof

Each area rests on a small number of samples. Patterns are signals to explore, not causal claims about health or behavior.

A research prototype

This tool reflects wastewater sampling from Durham County and is intended for exploration and public-health research, not personal dietary assessment.
